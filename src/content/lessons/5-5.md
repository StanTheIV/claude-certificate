# Design Human Review Workflows and Confidence Calibration

## Aggregate accuracy can hide a real problem

An extraction pipeline reporting "97% overall accuracy" sounds like a system ready to run with minimal oversight. But an aggregate number is an average across everything — every document type, every field. It can easily hide a segment that performs far worse than the headline suggests: a system might be 99%+ accurate on invoices but only 60% accurate on handwritten receipts, and still average out to 97% overall if invoices dominate the volume.

If you reduce human review based on the aggregate number alone, you will under-review exactly the segment that needs it most, because the aggregate never told you it was weak.

> **Key idea:** A single accuracy number describes the average case, not the worst case. Before trusting it to reduce oversight, ask "accurate for whom, on what?"

## Validate by document type and field before automating

Before reducing human review for any extraction category, break accuracy down by **document type** and by **field**, not just overall. A pipeline that extracts vendor name, invoice date, and line-item totals might be reliable on vendor name across all document types, but consistently weak on line-item totals for scanned (versus digitally native) documents.

This segmented validation is what tells you *where* it's actually safe to reduce human review and where it isn't — automating "extraction" as one monolithic category, based on one aggregate score, risks scaling up a hidden weak spot right along with the strong parts.

## Stratified random sampling for ongoing monitoring

Even after a category is validated as high-confidence, don't stop checking it. Implement **stratified random sampling**: regularly pull a random sample from within each segment (by document type, by field) — not just a random sample of *everything*, which would be dominated by whatever segment is highest-volume and could still miss a problem concentrated in a smaller segment.

Stratified sampling serves two purposes: it measures the ongoing error rate for extractions the system is confident about (confirming the confidence is still justified), and it helps detect **novel error patterns** — a new document layout, a new vendor format — before it silently degrades accuracy at scale.

> **Exam trap:** "Sample a large random batch of all extractions" sounds thorough, but if one document type is only 2% of volume, a flat random sample will rarely include enough of it to say anything reliable about its error rate. Stratifying by segment is what makes the sample meaningful.

## Field-level confidence, calibrated on labeled data

Rather than one confidence score per document, have the model output a **confidence score per extracted field**. Different fields on the same document can have very different reliability — a clearly printed invoice number might be near-certain, while a total that had to be inferred from a smudged subtotal and tax line is much less certain.

A raw confidence score from the model is not automatically trustworthy on its own. **Calibrate** the threshold using a labeled validation set: check what accuracy actually corresponds to a "confidence of 0.85" for this field and this document type, and set your human-review threshold based on that measured relationship — not on the number's face value.

## Routing rules: what goes to a human

With calibrated, field-level confidence in hand, route:

- **Low model confidence** on a field or document → human review.
- **Ambiguous or contradictory source documents** (two figures on the same document conflict, or the document is only partially legible) → human review, regardless of the confidence score, since the source material itself is the problem.
- **High confidence, validated-consistent segments** → automated processing, with the ongoing stratified sampling from the previous chunk as a safety net.

Because reviewer time is limited, this routing is also a prioritization mechanism: it concentrates scarce human attention on the cases most likely to be wrong or most likely to matter, instead of spreading review thin across everything uniformly.

## Exam lens: why the distractors lose

- **Trusting a 97% overall accuracy figure vs. validating by document type and field** — the aggregate can conceal a weak segment; only segmented analysis tells you it's actually safe to reduce review there.
- **A single random sample of all output vs. stratified random sampling by segment** — a flat sample under-represents low-volume, high-risk segments; stratification is what makes the measurement meaningful for each one.
- **Trusting the model's raw confidence score at face value vs. calibrating it on a labeled validation set** — an uncalibrated confidence number doesn't tell you what accuracy it actually corresponds to; calibration ties the number to measured outcomes.
- **Routing only by confidence score vs. also routing ambiguous/contradictory source documents to review** — a document can produce a falsely high confidence score even when the source material itself is unreliable; source ambiguity is a separate trigger from low confidence.
- **Reviewing everything uniformly vs. prioritizing limited reviewer capacity toward low-confidence and contradictory cases** — spreading fixed review capacity evenly wastes it on cases that were already reliable, at the cost of cases that needed the attention.
