# Preserve Information Provenance and Handle Uncertainty in Multi-Source Synthesis

## How attribution gets lost during summarization

When a research or analysis agent combines findings from multiple sources into a summary, it's easy for the link between a claim and where it came from to quietly disappear. A subagent might read a report and pass along "the AI music market is estimated at $3.2B" — true to the source, but stripped of *which* source, and *when* it was published. Once that link is gone, it usually cannot be reconstructed later; the next agent in the pipeline can only work with what it was given.

```
Bad:  "The AI music market is estimated at $3.2B." (No source, no year.)

Good: {
  "claim": "The AI music market is estimated at $3.2B.",
  "source_url": "https://example.com/report",
  "source_name": "Global AI Music Report 2024",
  "publication_date": "2024-06-15",
  "confidence": 0.9
}
```

This is a direct consequence of the general context-management lesson from 5.1 — structured data survives summarization better than prose — applied specifically to *who said this and when*.

## Structured claim-source mappings must survive synthesis

The fix is to require every subagent that produces findings to output them as structured **claim-source mappings**: the claim itself, the source URL or document name, the relevant excerpt, and ideally a confidence indicator. The synthesis agent's job is then to **preserve and merge** these mappings as it combines findings from multiple subagents — not to flatten them into unattributed prose along the way.

If the synthesis step drops the structure to produce a cleaner-reading paragraph, it has re-introduced the exact attribution loss the structured format was meant to prevent. Preserving provenance has to survive every hop in the pipeline, not just the first one.

## Conflicting statistics: annotate, don't pick

Credible sources sometimes disagree. When a document-analysis subagent finds two different values for the same claim, the wrong move is to silently choose one (which one — the higher number? the more recent-sounding source?) and pass only that forward. The correct move is to preserve **both** values, each with its own source attribution, and flag the conflict explicitly:

```json
{
  "claim": "Share of AI-generated music on streaming platforms",
  "values": [
    {"value": "12%", "source": "Spotify Annual Report 2024", "date": "2024-03", "methodology": "Automated classification"},
    {"value": "8%", "source": "Music Industry Association Survey", "date": "2024-07", "methodology": "Survey of 500 labels"}
  ],
  "conflict_detected": true,
  "possible_explanation": "Difference in methodology and time period"
}
```

Completing the document-analysis step this way — conflicting values included and explicitly annotated — lets the coordinator or a later synthesis step decide how to reconcile them, instead of having that decision made silently and irreversibly upstream, by whichever subagent happened to process the conflicting document first.

## Dates prevent false contradictions

Without publication or data-collection dates, two genuinely consistent findings can look like a contradiction:

```
Bad:  "Source A says 10%, source B says 15%. Contradiction."
Good: "Source A (2023) says 10%, source B (2024) says 15%. Likely +5% growth over a year."
```

Requiring subagents to include publication or data-collection dates in their structured outputs is what makes this correct temporal interpretation possible downstream. Without the date field, the synthesis agent has no way to tell a real conflict apart from a normal trend over time — it can only guess, and guessing here produces a report that either wrongly flags agreement as conflict or wrongly flags conflict as agreement.

## Structuring the report: established, contested, and rendered by type

Two more provenance-preserving practices belong in the final report itself:

**Separate established from contested findings.** Structure the report with explicit sections distinguishing findings that are well-supported and consistent across sources from findings that are contested or conflicting, preserving each source's original characterization and methodological context rather than blending everything into one uniform-confidence narrative.

**Render by content type, not one uniform format.** Don't force every kind of finding into the same shape:

- Financial data → tables
- News and qualitative analysis → prose
- Technical findings → structured lists
- Time series → chronological ordering

Forcing everything into prose, for example, makes a table of figures harder to scan and easier to misread; forcing everything into a table makes nuanced qualitative findings feel falsely precise. Matching the rendering to the content type keeps the report both accurate and usable.

## Exam lens: why the distractors lose

- **Passing along a clean, unattributed claim vs. a structured claim-source mapping** — a tidier-looking sentence is worse once the source and date are gone, because that loss usually can't be undone downstream.
- **Letting the synthesis step flatten mappings into prose for readability vs. preserving structure through the merge** — attribution loss can happen at *any* hop, including the last one; "it survived the first subagent" isn't enough.
- **Picking the "better" of two conflicting statistics vs. preserving both with attribution and flagging the conflict** — an arbitrary pick hides a real disagreement between credible sources and removes the coordinator's ability to reconcile it deliberately.
- **Treating two different numbers as a contradiction vs. checking their dates first** — without a date field, a normal trend over time and a genuine conflict are indistinguishable; requiring dates is what makes the distinction possible.
- **Rendering every finding as uniform prose vs. matching the format to content type** — a table of financial figures rewritten as a paragraph is harder to verify; forcing structure onto qualitative analysis makes it feel more certain than it is.
